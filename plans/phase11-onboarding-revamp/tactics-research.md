# Methods Research — The Staged Methods Sales & Consulting Actually Use

Not behavioral tips — methods: named stages, what happens in each, what must be
true to advance, what the customer needs at each stage. Comparison and our pick:
[ideas.md](./ideas.md).

Three kinds of thing get called "a method." Keep them apart:
- **Stage models** — ordered stages with gates (Sandler, Bowtie, Solution Selling, CS lifecycle, GDS, clinical method, Block)
- **Checklists** — facts to verify, no order (BANT, MEDDPICC, SPICED)
- **Conversation scripts** — an arc inside one conversation (Challenger, Maister's five steps, SPIN)

A full method needs one of each: a backbone, a gate discipline, a script.

## Part 1 — Sales methods

### MEDDPICC (checklist used as stage-gates)
Eight facts to verify: **M**etrics (quantified outcome expected) · **E**conomic
buyer · **D**ecision criteria · **D**ecision process · **P**aper process ·
**I**dentified pain · **C**hampion · **C**ompetition. Teams bolt it onto a
pipeline: you may not advance a stage until specific letters are documented with
evidence, scored 1–3, and **re-scored continuously** — a qualified deal can be
disqualified at any time. Core claim: deals die from unverified assumptions.
- Pro: forecast honesty; forces early disqualification; continuous re-check.
- Con: seller-side — tracks what *we* know, gives the buyer nothing; becomes
  box-ticking; no guidance on how to talk; nothing post-sale.
- Fit for us: not a backbone. Steal one idea: **trust facts decay — re-verify,
  and stepping down voluntarily builds trust.**

### Sandler Submarine (stage model, order enforced)
Seven compartments, each sealed behind you so it can't resurface later:
1. **Bonding & rapport** — peer stance, no neediness. Exit: guard down.
2. **Up-front contract** — before anything: agenda, time, both roles, and the
   allowed outcomes *including no*. Exit: mutual deal on what this interaction
   decides. Every later meeting gets its own contract.
3. **Pain** — the pain funnel down to owned, quantified pain. No pain → qualify
   out. Exit: pain stated in their words with its cost.
4. **Budget** — willing and able (money, time) before any solution shown.
5. **Decision** — who decides, how, when; pre-agree what happens if the demo
   solves the pain.
6. **Fulfillment** — present *only* against the sealed pains/budget/decision.
   Close is a check, not an event.
7. **Post-sell** — invite them to back out now; kills buyer's remorse.
Why the order: presenting before pain/budget/decision are sealed gives free
consulting and invites "think it over"; sealed budget can't return as an
objection at close.
- Pro: built exactly for skeptical small-business owners; the up-front contract
  is the best trust device in the literature; never needy.
- Con: one-conversation-thread; weak post-sale; feels gimmicky if mechanical.
- Fit for us: **the gate mechanic.** Every step boundary is an up-front
  contract; "no is a fine outcome" everywhere.

### Winning by Design — SPICED + Bowtie (full-lifecycle stage model)
The funnel is only the left half; the right half is as big or bigger, because in
recurring revenue **most value is won after the signature**. Stages with exits:
1. **Awareness** — exit: pain acknowledged in the customer's words.
2. **Education** — diagnose like a doctor. Exit: **impact quantified and owned
   by the customer** (their number).
3. **Selection** — prove the solution reaches the impact. Exit: decision process
   mapped, critical event dated.
4. **Commit** — exit: signature *plus success criteria carried over the wall*.
5. **Onboard** — exit: **first impact** reached fast; failure here predicts
   churn regardless of sale quality.
6. **Adoption / impact** — exit: the impact number from stage 2 measured and
   acknowledged. Recurring impact = recurring revenue.
7. **Expansion** — earned by proven impact; loops back to stage 1 for new scope.
Operational rules: advance one stage at a time, forward only; no silent
regression — a stalled deal closes with a stated reason.
- Pro: the only sales model whose thesis is **the sale is not the goal, measured
  impact is**; exit criteria are customer-verifiable facts; one language across
  the whole lifecycle.
- Con: needs discipline at the handoff; impact numbers are hard early.
- Fit for us: **the backbone shape.** "Permission granted" is a midpoint; the
  ladder runs on delivered, measured impact.

### Solution Selling (stage model — invented the verifiable milestone)
Pipeline letters T→S→D→C→B→A→W, each with completion criteria. The gates that
matter: **pain admitted** (no admitted pain, no opportunity) → buying vision →
the **Sponsor Letter** (a written summary of pain, vision, next steps that the
*buyer* confirms) → access to power → a **mutual evaluation plan**. 
- Pro: the buyer-confirmed written milestone and the mutual plan — direct
  ancestors of everything modern.
- Con: scripted question models feel formulaic; assumes the buyer needs us to
  diagnose; heavy paperwork.
- Fit for us: steal the Sponsor Letter (our reflect-back that the owner
  confirms) and the mutual plan (what I do / what you get me).

### Force Management — verifiable outcomes (gate doctrine, method-agnostic)
A stage may only advance on an **observable action or artifact produced by the
customer** — agreed to the problem statement in writing, introduced the
economic buyer, completed validation. Seller activities ("sent proposal", "gave
demo") prove nothing. Managers inspect outcomes, not activity.
- Fit for us: **the advancement law.** Luna's stage/autonomy advances only on
  owner actions — confirmed, approved, delegated — never on her own activity
  counts. (Matches curiosity's server-computed-truth rule exactly.)

### Challenger (conversation script, order enforced)
Warmer (insight, not small talk) → **reframe** (a problem they hadn't seen) →
rational drowning (numbers until status quo is indefensible) → emotional impact
→ new way (vendor-neutral) → solution last. Lead with product and you're a
commodity; lead with the reframe and the product arrives pre-differentiated.
- Pro: only script built for "no decision" / status-quo inertia.
- Con: tension-based — risky with an already-skeptical buyer; per-conversation
  only, no multi-day structure.
- Fit for us: small doses. The reframe is legal only when **earned from their
  own data** (our free-finding step), never asserted.

### BANT (checklist, mostly retired)
Budget-Authority-Need-Timeline. Fell out of favor because it measures seller
convenience, extracts and gives nothing back, assumes budget pre-exists (impact
creates budget), assumes one authority, and is asked once then never re-checked.
- Fit for us: none, beyond the cautionary tale — **a method that interrogates
  without giving value back is exactly what we must not build.**

## Part 2 — Consulting & adoption methods

### Peter Block — Flawless Consulting (5 phases)
Premise: a consultant is someone with expertise but **no control** — influence
only. That is precisely Luna's position. Cardinal finding: **most consulting
failures are contracting failures discovered later.**
1. **Entry & contracting** — negotiate wants explicitly, both directions: scope,
   deliverable, what the consultant needs *from the client* (access, data, a
   decision-maker in the room), time, feedback. Name resistance in neutral words
   ("you seem reluctant to share the sales data — can we talk about that?") —
   resistance is fear of losing control; it dissolves by being voiced, never by
   being argued with. Responsibility split 50/50. A "no" here is a success.
2. **Discovery & dialogue** — build an independent view of the problem, *with*
   the client in the inquiry so discovery itself creates commitment.
3. **Feedback & the decision to act** — funnel to the few things the client can
   act on, in ten-year-old language; present, then stop; the *client* decides.
   No resistance in the room = they're not taking it seriously.
4. **Engagement & implementation** — change chosen by people, not installed on
   them.
5. **Extension, recycle, or termination** — an explicit choice; every extension
   is a fresh contracting conversation.
- Pro: the only classic method for influence-without-authority; makes the
  client's fear of losing control a first-class object.
- Con: project-shaped; assumes rich two-way conversation; can read as stalling.
- Fit for us: **the contracting script and the stance** (50/50, name the fear,
  no is success, recycle loop = continuous relationship).

### McKinsey / Big-4 engagement lifecycle
LOP (proposal already containing a day-1 hypothesis) → kickoff (workplan,
client counterparts, steering committee) → diagnostic (hypothesis-driven, only
the analysis that proves/kills a branch; answer-first SCR communication) →
**progress checkpoints** (SteerCo can stop, re-scope, approve — the client
spends real authority) → final readout (**never a surprise — everything
pre-wired 1:1 first**) → implementation.
- Pro: answer-first communication; the no-surprises rule; checkpoints as real
  gates.
- Con: big-diagnostic-before-value — a skeptic paying per action won't fund it;
  built for clients with governance apparatus.
- Fit for us: three steals — answer-first reporting, pre-wire everything
  consequential, a fixed checkpoint where the owner decides.

### Prosci ADKAR (adoption method — models the CUSTOMER's state)
Five outcomes that must occur **inside the adopter, in order, no skipping**:
**A**wareness (can state why the change is needed) → **D**esire (has personally
chosen to engage — the one element you cannot do *to* someone) → **K**nowledge
(knows how — training lands here, third; delivered before Desire it produces
resentment) → **A**bility (demonstrated performance, with tolerance for early
stumbles) → **R**einforcement (visible wins keep it from decaying).
**Barrier-point rule:** score each element in order; the first weak one is the
barrier; aim all effort there and only there — working later elements while an
earlier one is unmet is wasted or counterproductive.
- Pro: the only framework that models where the *person* is stuck; explains why
  demos flop (pitching Knowledge at missing Desire); measurable.
- Con: individual-level only; soft scores; says nothing about what to build.
- Fit for us: **the diagnostic inner loop** — Luna asks "which letter is the
  owner stuck on?" and her next message targets that letter only.

### Kotter's 8 steps (org change)
Urgency → coalition → vision → communicate ×10 → remove barriers → **generate
short-term wins** → don't declare victory → anchor in culture.
- Fit for us: two principles only — **engineered small wins are the fuel that
  buys the next mandate**, and never declare victory: each win is immediately
  invested in proposing the next, slightly larger scope. The rest is org-scale.

### SaaS Customer Success lifecycle (continuous-relationship stage model)
The only method natively shaped like our economics: subscription, provider dies
when the client stops perceiving value.
1. **Kickoff** — restate why they bought; agree success criteria in *their*
   business language; write the **success plan** and a **mutual action plan**
   (tasks with owners on both sides — the client's obligations explicit).
2. **Implementation** — minimize customer lift; every ball left in the
   customer's court extends time-to-value and creates churn risk.
3. **First value** — the **first value milestone**, defined per customer at
   kickoff, in customer terms (not "training complete"). **Time-to-first-value
   is the strongest early predictor of renewal.** Exit: the customer has the
   outcome and acknowledges it.
4. **Adoption** — habitual use, health telemetry, proactive unblocking before
   they complain.
5. **Expansion & renewal** — the **QBR**: not a status meeting — a value recap
   (outcomes vs plan, quantified) plus re-contracting of next-period goals.
   Renewal as the natural conclusion of demonstrated value. Loops to stage 1.
- Pro: continuous by design; value re-proven on a rhythm; obligations mutual
  and written; one forcing metric (TTV).
- Con: QBRs degrade into slideware; health scores get gamed; expansion motive
  smells if visible.
- Fit for us: **the backbone.** Same shape as the Bowtie's right half, told
  from the delivery side.

### The clinical method (per-action safety protocol, order legally enforced)
Intake/history (the history alone yields ~80% of diagnoses — being heard *is*
the diagnostic) → examination → differential diagnosis → **diagnosis
communicated in lay terms before any treatment talk** → **treatment plan +
informed consent** (nature, risks, benefits, alternatives *including doing
nothing* — consent is decision-specific and revocable) → treatment (only what
was consented; scope change = new consent) → follow-up (the diagnosis is never
fully closed).
Why enforced: liability attaches to skipped steps, not just bad outcomes —
treating without consent is battery *even if the treatment succeeds*; "not
documented = not done."
- Fit for us: **the per-action protocol.** Standing autonomy = a repeat
  prescription: consented once for a defined class, always revocable.

### GDS phases (evidence-gated graduation: Discovery → Alpha → Beta → Live)
Discovery (understand, build nothing; **"stop, don't build this" is a success
exit**) → Alpha (cheap prototypes of >1 approach, riskiest assumptions tested)
→ Beta (real service, limited scale, real users, metrics) → Live (never "done";
continuous improvement or deliberate retirement). Each gate is a formal
assessment against published criteria.
- Pro: purest prove-it-to-advance model; cheap phases de-risk expensive ones;
  no-go legitimized.
- Con: gate theater; built for teams, not relationships.
- Fit for us: **the autonomy ladder per capability class** — watch → propose →
  act-with-approval → standing, each notch gated on evidence shown to the owner.

### Maister — the five-step trust conversation (+ the trust equation)
Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-orientation — reliability
is the only component that *requires repeated interactions*, which is why trust
takes time and why kept micro-promises are the substrate. The conversation
method, order enforced, at every scale from one exchange to a whole
relationship:
**Engage** (earn the conversation with something specific to them) → **Listen**
(until they say "exactly" — this *earns the right to comment*) → **Frame** (the
root issue named freshly, offered with permission, as a hypothesis) →
**Envision** (what does fixed look like, before any method talk) → **Commit**
(specific mutual next steps, expectations managed *down* to what's real).
Advice given before understanding is demonstrated gets rejected regardless of
quality.
- Fit for us: **the conversational grammar** for every consequential exchange,
  and the design constraint: never argue for our own autonomy on our own
  behalf — only as owner cost/benefit with a genuine "or not."

## Part 3 — Implementation partners & system integrators

We are not only advising — we build a system and then run it. That's a third
world with its own methods.

### SAP Activate (Discover → Prepare → Explore → Realize → Deploy → Run)
The signature move is **fit-to-standard** (Explore phase): the partner *demos
the working standard system* and asks "does this fit?" — requirements by
demonstration, capturing only deltas, instead of interviewing about wants. A
**quality gate** ends every phase (go/no-go against a checklist); UAT sign-off
ends Realize; hypercare sits inside Deploy; Run is handed to the customer's
support organization.
- Pro: default-first kills requirements churn; explicit gates.
- Con: gates get rubber-stamped; delta creep; Run handover fails when the
  customer has no support org.
- Fit for us: **fit-to-standard transfers directly** — Luna shows a working
  default automation and captures corrections. The Run handover does *not*
  transfer: Luna IS the support org; Run stays hers.

### Salesforce partner delivery (Discovery → Design → Build → UAT → Go-live → Hypercare → Adoption)
The decisive gate is **UAT sign-off**: business users validate real workflows,
then sign that the system does what was agreed. After sign-off, new issues are
change requests, not defects — "done" becomes contractual and mutual.
- Pro: perfect scale match for small business; UAT sign-off creates the trust
  boundary; hypercare standard.
- Con: UAT run by the project team instead of real users makes it meaningless;
  partner leaves after hypercare → shelfware.
- Fit for us: **per-automation UAT** — "here are 10 real emails and the drafts
  I'd have sent — approve?" Minutes, not weeks, but always an explicit owner
  action. This is the autonomy license.

### Hypercare (the go-live aftercare window)
A time-bound elevated-support state right after go-live: build team stays on,
fast fix loop, daily digests. Exists for defect flush (real load surfaces what
tests missed) and trust (first-weeks experience decides adoption). **Exit on
criteria, never calendar**: zero critical issues, one full business cycle
completed, tickets at normal level — customer-signed.
- Fit for us: **hypercare miniaturizes per automation** — every new automation
  runs in elevated scrutiny (outputs double-checked, tight alerts, daily note)
  until N consecutive correct runs + one full weekly cycle + zero owner
  corrections, then auto-promotes with a notification ("email triage left
  hypercare: 47 handled, 0 corrections").

### Big-SI deliver→operate (Accenture ADM, Deloitte Operate)
Gates tied to **measurable outcomes, not documents**; monitoring and SLAs must
exist *before* go-live (operations has its own readiness gate); benefits
tracked after launch, continuously. Deloitte's Operate model: the SI never
hands over — it runs the function as a managed service.
- Fit for us: Luna is inherently a deliver→operate firm. Steal: **no automation
  goes live until she can detect, diagnose, and stop it** (monitoring + SLO +
  kill switch first), and value telemetry reported to the owner continuously.

### MSP onboarding & operations (the perpetual-operations grammar)
Transition-in (inventory, access, documentation) → go-live → steady state
under a **service catalog** (exactly what is and isn't covered, per-service
SLAs by severity) → **QBRs** (first one scheduled during onboarding) →
contractual continual improvement (proactive recommendations are an
obligation, not an upsell).
- Pro: the only model designed for perpetually operating many small things for
  small businesses.
- Con: SLA-watching replaces outcome-thinking; QBRs skipped for small accounts
  → churn.
- Fit for us: **the steady-state backbone** — an owner-visible registry of
  every automation Luna operates (scope, status, SLO), plus a review rhythm
  where improvement proposals are part of the deal.

### Microsoft FastTrack (Envision → Onboard → Drive Value)
The only framework centered *after* deployment: success = active usage per
workload, not delivery. Detect shelfware (system runs, users ignore it),
intervene, expand from working beachheads.
- Fit for us: **adoption telemetry as a first-class alarm** — "automation runs
  but the owner overrides or ignores its output" must page Luna the way an
  error would.

### Why implementations fail after go-live (ERP research)
55–75% of ERP implementations miss expected ROI — and the failure happens
*after* go-live: adoption cliff (usage peaks at launch, reverts at first
friction), rushed UAT, no post-launch optimization. Human factors ~6× more
impactful than technical ones. Partners answer with 30/60/90-day reviews,
usage audits, retraining triggered by telemetry, phased rollouts.
- Fit for us: the why behind everything above — operating what we build makes
  us immune to the orphaned-system problem *only if we watch adoption, not
  just uptime*.

### The implementation skeleton these sources support
Discover by demo (fit-to-standard) → micro-build → **owner UAT sign-off on
real samples** [gate: the autonomy license] → go-live with operate-readiness
self-check [gate: monitorable + killable] → **hypercare state, exit on
criteria** [gate: auto-promote, owner notified] → steady state under catalog +
SLOs → recurring review driving improvements and the next go-live.

## Part 4 — Operating the system: run, mistakes, trust repair, improvement

We keep running what we build. That's a fourth world — IT operations, site
reliability, and service recovery.

### ITIL (trimmed to what a small operator keeps)
- **Incident vs problem** — separate clocks: restore service first (incident),
  investigate the cause separately (problem). The midnight call = incident
  (stop the automation now, apologize); "calls have no time-of-day check" =
  problem (fix the design).
- **Three change types**: *standard* (pre-authorized, no per-instance approval)
  · *normal* (needs approval, scaled to risk) · *emergency* (act, document
  after). The practical move: a growing **standard-change catalog** — the
  pre-approved action list, which *shrinks* after an incident and regrows as
  trust returns. This is our autonomy ladder in operations language.
- **Continual improvement register**: a running, prioritized list of
  improvement ideas with status — improvement made legible.
- **Watermelon SLA warning**: green dashboards outside, red customer
  experience inside — measure what the customer feels, not internals.

### SRE (Google)
- **SLOs + error budgets**: pick indicators the owner cares about, set a
  target below 100%, and spend the gap on change speed. Budget burned →
  **pre-agreed freeze**: stop expanding, run only the proven set until X clean
  days pass. The post-mistake slowdown becomes principled and time-bounded,
  not vibes. Some SLOs are hard invariants with a **zero** budget ("no
  customer contact outside business hours" — one breach = freeze).
- **Blameless postmortem**: impact (quantified) · timeline · root cause (the
  system, never the actor) · contributing factors · **detection** ("the
  customer told us" is itself a finding — monitoring gap) · action items
  split *prevent / detect / mitigate*, each with owner and deadline. "Be more
  careful" is a failed postmortem — each recurrence-class item must become a
  **machine-enforced rule plus a test that attempts the failure and proves
  it's now impossible**.
- **Toil rule**: track repeated manual work; automate the top item — the
  formal channel from operating to improving.

### Service recovery research (the paradox)
- **Service recovery paradox**: a failure followed by excellent recovery can
  leave the customer *more* trusting than no failure — recovery is a vivid
  demonstration routine service never provides.
- **It's one-shot**: after a *second* similar failure, satisfaction falls
  below pre-failure even with perfect recovery (Maxham & Netemeyer 2002). So
  the guardrail matters more than the apology.
- Recovery steps, in order of leverage: **self-report before the customer
  finds out** (speed is the strongest lever) → own it and apologize → explain
  the cause honestly → fix + proportional gesture → **show the systemic
  change** (attacks the "it will happen again" belief) → follow up.

### Trust repair (organizational psychology)
- Violations split into **competence** (failed at the skill) and **integrity**
  (dishonesty). For competence failures, a full-ownership apology repairs
  well; hiding, minimizing, or quietly editing the record converts a
  competence failure into an integrity one — and **trust broken by deception
  never fully recovers, while trust broken by unreliability does**
  (Schweitzer 2006).
- **Substantive beats verbal**: penance and *self-imposed binding constraints
  on your own future behavior* repair trust better than any apology — they
  re-demonstrate trustworthiness instead of asserting it. An agent's
  constraint is uniquely credible: it's a rule in a file plus a test, not a
  promise.
- Then: demonstrated clean behavior over a defined window, periodically
  evidenced ("34 days, 212 actions, zero policy exceptions").

### Guardrails / policy-as-code + progressive delivery
- Boundaries as machine-evaluated rules between intent and execution: quiet
  hours in *customer-local* time, spend caps, rate/blast-radius limits,
  channel allowlists (email yes, phone approval-only), approval thresholds
  (irreversible, expensive, or never-done-before → human). Two properties:
  **default-deny for novel action types** and **fail-closed** (timezone
  lookup fails → the call does not happen).
- The policy file is human-readable, versioned, and testable — the owner can
  open their boundaries and read them.
- **Progressive delivery**: every automation behind a kill switch; new
  behavior canaried on a small slice first; rollback automatic on error
  signal. Blast radius capped by construction.

### Kaizen / PDCA / Toyota kata (the peacetime rhythm)
Many small improvements on a rhythm, proposed by whoever is closest to the
work: current condition → measurable target → experiment with a **predicted
result stated before running** → check actual vs predicted → standardize what
worked. Approvals are small, fast, and frequent by design.
- Fit for us: Luna proposes one micro-improvement a week (predicted benefit +
  cost, reversible, one-tap approve); the owner is the coach.

### Proactive service reviews without smelling like upsell (MSP/vCIO)
The review is a look-back (promised vs delivered, incidents and how handled),
a health report, a ranked roadmap — and **one decision ask**, not a menu.
The discipline that keeps it advisory: anchor every proposal in a goal the
owner stated *in their own words* earlier · quantify both sides in their units
· **include downsells** ("turn this off, it's not paying for itself" — the
strongest credibility signal) · never bundle proposals with incident recovery
· "everything ran, nothing to change" is itself a valuable report.

## What the two worlds agree on

1. The relationship advances through **stages with gates**, and the gate is
   always a **customer action**, never provider activity.
2. **Diagnosis is confirmed by the customer before anything is prescribed** —
   Sponsor Letter, Block's feedback decision, the communicated diagnosis,
   Maister's Frame. Every method has this step; ours currently bans it.
3. **A contract precedes every step** — up-front contract, Block's contracting,
   informed consent, the mutual action plan. Cost, outcome, obligations both
   ways, and a cheap no.
4. **The first value must land fast and be acknowledged** — TTV, first impact,
   short-term wins, the quick win.
5. **The sale is a midpoint** — Bowtie right half, CS lifecycle, Block's
   recycle. Impact measured against the number agreed at diagnosis.
6. **Regression is legitimate and explicit** — disqualify, de-scope, retire;
   silent stalling is the only forbidden state.
